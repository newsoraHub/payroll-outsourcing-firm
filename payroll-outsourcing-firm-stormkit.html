Payroll Firm Review UK

How to judge a payroll provider before you hand over the pay run

Choosing a Payroll Outsourcing Firm in the UK

A good payroll outsourcing firm takes the whole pay run off an employer's desk and returns payslips that staff can trust, filings that arrive on time, and records that stand up to a check. A poor one does the arithmetic while leaving the employer to chase dates, fix errors and answer staff questions alone. This guide explains what such a firm should actually do, how to compare providers on equal terms, and which questions separate a careful provider from a cheap one.

What a Payroll Firm Really Does

Payroll looks simple from the outside. Staff work, pay is calculated, money arrives in the bank. The work behind that result is a chain of small duties, and a break in any link causes a visible failure. A late change, a wrong code, or a missed filing each reaches staff as a wrong payslip or a late payment, and each one costs trust that is slow to rebuild. A payroll firm collects hours and changes, checks them against contracts, calculates gross pay, tax, national insurance and pension, prepares payslips, pays staff or prepares the payment file, files returns with HMRC, and keeps records that can be shown later. It also answers the questions that follow, such as why a figure changed, what a code means, or when a correction will land.

Why Employers Hand Pay Over

Employers hand pay over for three reasons, and each one points to a different kind of provider. The first is time. A pay run eats hours that a small team would rather spend on customers, and the work grows with every starter, leaver and change. The second is risk. Tax rules, pension duties and filing deadlines change, and a missed filing brings a penalty that costs more than the service would have done. The third is trust. Staff judge a business by the payslip, and one wrong period damages confidence that takes months to rebuild.

Payroll in the UK, in Plain Terms

UK payroll has a fixed shape that every firm must follow. Pay is calculated for each period, tax and national insurance are deducted under PAYE, pension contributions are taken under auto enrolment, and the employer reports pay and deductions to HMRC on or before each pay day through Real Time Information. Payslips must show the information staff are entitled to see, including hours where pay varies by time worked. An employer who cannot explain a payslip figure in plain words should ask the firm to explain it first, before staff have to ask. Year end brings further returns and statements. A competent firm builds its calendar backwards from each deadline, collects changes early, and leaves room to check before anything is filed. An employer should ask to see that calendar, and should expect the same dates to appear in every conversation.

Services a Firm Should Offer

The core service is the pay run itself, but the value of a firm sits in the services around it. Starter and leaver handling, contract changes, absence and leave, overtime and bonuses, expenses and benefits, pension enrolment and opt outs, court orders and student loans, and year end work should all be covered in one routine. Reporting should include cost by team, starters and leavers, absence, and a clear summary of what was filed and when. Support should give staff one route to ask questions and one answer that covers the whole picture. An employer should list the services it actually needs, including the awkward ones such as mid period changes and corrections, and should ask the firm to confirm each one in writing.

How Pricing Usually Works

Pricing for payroll is usually built from a fixed monthly charge plus a charge per payslip, with extras for additional runs, corrections, year end work and reports. That shape is fair only when every element is visible before the contract is signed. An employer should ask for the full list of charges, including the cost of an extra run, a correction after payslips have gone out, a change to the pay calendar, and support beyond a stated limit. Compare providers on the same basis, using the same headcount, the same number of runs, and the same list of extras, over a full year. A firm that will not give a written price breakdown is telling the employer something important about how it will behave later.

Accuracy and the Checking Routine

Accuracy is not a promise, it is a routine, and an employer should be able to describe the routine of any firm it hires. A good routine has four parts. First, changes arrive in one approved list, with dates, so nothing is taken from a passing remark. Second, one person enters or imports the change and a second person checks the pay result before payslips are released. Third, the firm compares the current run with the previous run and explains every material difference. Fourth, corrections are recorded with the reason, so the same error is not repeated. An employer should ask how many checks happen before payslips go out, who performs them, and what happens when a check finds a problem close to the cut off. A firm that checks twice and files once will almost always beat a firm that files first and corrects later.

Deadlines, Cut Offs and the Calendar

A payroll calendar is a promise to staff, and the cut off is the date that makes the promise possible. The calendar should state the cut off for changes, the date pay is calculated, the date payslips are released, the pay date, and the filing date, for every period of the year. The firm should publish one calendar that the employer and the firm both use, and should treat the cut off as a real deadline. Late changes need a named approver and a written record, so the same delay can be prevented next time. When a firm misses its own calendar, the employer should ask for the reason in writing and for the change that will prevent a repeat.

Compliance and HMRC Filings

Compliance is the part of payroll where the employer carries the legal duty even when a firm does the work. The firm must file Real Time Information submissions on time, apply the correct tax codes, operate student loan and court order deductions correctly, and keep records for the required period. The employer should ask which filings the firm makes, when each one is made, and how the employer can see that a filing succeeded. A filing confirmation, a summary of what was submitted, and a clear route for corrections should be standard, not extras. The employer should also ask what happens when HMRC queries a figure, and who speaks to HMRC on the employer's behalf.

Pensions and Auto Enrolment

Pensions add a second payroll inside the first, and a firm must handle both without confusing them. Auto enrolment requires the employer to assess staff, enrol those who qualify, take the correct contributions, and keep evidence of every step. Opt outs, postponement, re enrolment and changes to pensionable pay each need a date and a record. A firm should explain its pension routine in plain words, including how new starters are assessed, how contributions are calculated, and how the pension provider receives the payment file. The employer should check the first pension run after starting with a firm, because pension errors are slow to surface and hard to unwind. A clear pension summary for each period, showing who was assessed and what was paid, is a basic expectation.

Data Protection and Security

A payroll firm holds the most sensitive information a business keeps, including names, addresses, bank details, tax codes and absence notes. The employer should agree the data rules in writing before any data moves. The agreement should state where data is stored, who can access it, how access is removed when staff change roles, how long records are kept, and what happens if data is lost or seen by the wrong person. Transfers of data need care, and payroll files should never travel by unsecured email as a matter of routine. The employer should ask how the firm checks its own access, how often access is reviewed, and how a person can ask to see their own record.

Support for Staff and Managers

Staff questions are the daily test of a payroll service. A good firm gives staff one route to ask, answers in plain words, and covers the whole picture, including the HR fact behind a pay figure. Managers need a short routine that fits the working week, with one place to confirm hours, overtime and changes, and one person to chase when information is missing. Support hours, response times and the route for urgent queries should be written into the agreement. An employer should test support before signing, by asking a real question about a real scenario, and judging the answer on speed, clarity and completeness.

Service Levels and What Good Looks Like

A service level agreement turns good intentions into measurable promises. It should state the cut off, the checking routine, the payslip release date, the filing date, the response time for queries, and the time allowed for corrections. It should also state what happens when a promise is missed, and how the employer will be told. Numbers should be realistic. A promise to answer every query within an hour is less useful than a promise to answer routine queries within one working day and urgent queries within four hours, kept every time. A firm that will not put service levels in writing is asking the employer to trust it without proof.

A payroll firm earns trust one period at a time. The calendar is kept, the figures match what was agreed, and questions get one clear answer.

Reports an Employer Should Receive

Reports turn payroll from a monthly task into a source of useful information. A monthly pack should include payroll cost by team, starters and leavers, absence, overtime, and a summary of filings made and taxes paid. Definitions should be agreed first, so headcount means the same thing in every report and a leaver counts in the same period in each one. The pack should arrive on one date, in one place, without the employer having to chase it. An employer should ask for a sample pack before signing, and should check that the figures can be traced back to the pay run without a separate explanation.

Switching Provider Without Disruption

Switching payroll provider is the moment when most errors happen, because records move between systems and old habits meet new routines. A careful switch has a named lead on each side, a short plan with dates, a complete transfer of records, and double checks on the first two runs under the new firm. Records to transfer include employee details, pay history, tax codes, pension details, leave balances and any outstanding deductions. The old provider should confirm in writing what has been handed over, and the new firm should confirm that every record has been checked. Payslips should be checked against the previous period, line by line, before they are released.

Contracts, Notice and Exit Terms

The contract should be read as carefully as the service description, because the exit terms matter as much as the entry terms. Notice periods, charges for leaving early, the cost of data extraction, and the format in which records are returned should all be stated plainly. An employer should never accept a contract that makes leaving expensive while making service levels vague. Data return is the most important exit term of all. The employer owns its payroll history, and the contract should guarantee its return in a usable format, within a stated time, at a stated cost or at no cost.

Questions to Ask Before You Sign

A short set of questions, asked of every provider, makes comparison honest. Ask how changes are collected and checked, and who checks the run before payslips go out. Ask for the calendar, the filing routine, and the evidence that filings succeeded. Ask how pensions are handled, how data is protected, and how staff questions are answered. Ask for the full price breakdown, the service levels in writing, and a sample monthly pack. Ask how a correction is handled, how a late change is approved, and how the employer leaves if the service disappoints. Ask to speak to a client of similar size, and ask what went wrong for that client and how it was fixed.

Final Thoughts

Choosing a payroll provider is a decision about trust, routine and evidence. The firm should show a clear calendar, a checking routine with a second pair of eyes, a filing routine with proof, a pension routine with dates, and data rules agreed in writing. Pricing should be complete, service levels should be written down, and reports should arrive on one date without chasing. An employer testing a provider should use a short checklist. First, can the firm describe its checking routine in one minute. Second, can it show the calendar and the filing evidence. Third, can it explain pensions and data protection in plain words. Fourth, can it give a full price breakdown and a sample pack. Fifth, can it explain how corrections, late changes and leaving work. If each answer is clear, the base is strong. If any answer is vague, that point is the place to press harder before signing.

Starting well matters more than starting fast. Agree the calendar, agree the change routine, and run the first period with extra checks before judging the service. Tell staff what is changing and who to ask, and ask the firm to show the checks on the first two runs. Keep a short log of promises made and promises kept, because that log is the fairest record when the contract comes up for review. Over time, a good firm should feel quiet and steady, with payslips that match what was agreed, filings that arrive on time, and answers that arrive once and arrive right. That steady routine, period after period, is what an employer is really paying for.
